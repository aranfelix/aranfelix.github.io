Executive summary

I analysed 307,511 labelled Home Credit applications to examine recorded repayment difficulty, the completeness of related credit-history records and the performance of statistical prediction models. I integrated application and historical tables using SQL Server, checked key relationships and repayment chronology, and prepared 34 predictors for modelling in Python. Of the labelled applications, 24,825 (8.07%) carried the source-defined payment-difficulty outcome. The analysis compared an application-only logistic model, a logistic model enriched with credit history, and a compact gradient-boosting model. The final evaluation used 61,503 previously held-out labelled applications, separate from the original Kaggle competition’s 48,744 unlabelled applications. The selected gradient-boosting model achieved 0.7552 ROC-AUC and 0.2455 average precision on that held-out sample. The results describe historical predictive discrimination and review prioritisation; they do not represent a tested lending policy or a current Kenyan credit-risk estimate.

Historical repayment records provided additional context beyond the application form. I distinguished 13,605,401 recorded payment entries from the number of distinct observed instalment keys, checked payment dates and recorded amounts, and audited historical records that could not be matched to their expected parent tables. Under a retrospective top-10% review scenario, the selected model identified 1,656 of 4,965 positive cases in the held-out test sample, equivalent to 33.35% recall. Reviewing the top 20% identified 2,577 cases, or 51.90% recall. These are capacity-based model summaries, not evidence that automated approvals or rejections would improve real-world outcomes. Subgroup results also showed differences in ranking, selection and calibration that warrant investigation without establishing discrimination or fairness. The linked report documents the source definitions, audits, validation process and remaining limitations.

Questions this research addresses

Credit risk

How common was the source-defined payment-difficulty outcome in the labelled historical applications?

Repayment history

What do recorded payments and observed instalments reveal about repayment timing and data coverage?

Prediction

Do historical credit and repayment indicators improve model performance beyond application information?

Review and subgroups

How much of the positive-case population can a fixed review capacity identify, and how does performance vary across groups?

Key findings

Payment difficulty appeared in 8.07% of labelled applications. There were 24,825 source-defined positive outcomes among 307,511 labelled applications. This is a modelled payment-difficulty indicator, not a verified measurement of loan loss or a forecast of defaults in Kenya.

Historical information improved predictive performance. ROC-AUC rose from 0.7384 for application-only logistic regression to 0.7514 for enriched logistic regression. The selected compact gradient-boosting model achieved 0.7552. These differences describe performance on the locked historical test set, not a guaranteed improvement in production lending.

Review capacity changed the number of positive cases found. Ranking the test sample by selected-model risk scores placed 1,656 positive cases within the highest-scored 10% of applications. The highest-scored 20% contained 2,577. Both scenarios use historical outcomes and assumed review capacities, rather than operational loan-decision rules.

Repayment records require careful denominator choices. The historical data contained 13,605,401 payment entries. Of the 13,602,496 dated entries, approximately 68.44% were recorded early, 23.13% on time and 8.43% late. A payment entry is not the same as a unique scheduled instalment, and observed amounts cannot establish full repayment coverage for wholly unobserved instalments.

Subgroup comparisons raised questions for further audit. For example, the selected model’s held-out ROC-AUC was approximately 0.7320 for applicants under 30 and 0.7000 for those aged 60 or older. Differences in risk distributions, selection rates and subgroup sizes complicate interpretation. These summaries alone cannot establish discrimination, fairness or deployment readiness.

The gradient-boosting configuration was selected before the reserved test set was evaluated. Its advantage over the enriched logistic model was modest, so the results should not be treated as proof that a more complex model will always perform better.

Repayment behaviour and review capacity

The historical payment analysis kept separate denominators for payment entries, dated entries, observed instalment keys and assessed amount coverage. Of 12,951,918 observed instalment keys, the available amount information covered approximately 99.95% of those observed keys. This figure does not measure coverage of instalments absent from the source records. Among covered observed instalments, approximately 91.48% had their recorded amount covered by the due date and 8.52% after the due date. These statements concern observed historical records and depend on the study’s amount-reconciliation rules.

The model’s prioritisation results provide a different view of performance. In the held-out sample, reviewing 6,151 highest-scored applications (the top 10%) would identify 1,656 positive outcomes: 26.92% precision and 33.35% recall. Reviewing 12,301 applications (the top 20%) would identify 2,577, yielding 20.95% precision and 51.90% recall. The exercise illustrates the trade-off between review effort and observed positive-case coverage. It does not estimate the financial return, cost or fairness of a real lending intervention.

Data and methods

I used the historical Home Credit Default Risk competition data, first released in 2018. The labelled application file provided 307,511 records, while a separate original competition test file contained 48,744 applications without the modelling outcome. I integrated eight analytical source tables in SQL Server, audited relationships and unmatched historical records, and verified repayment chronology. The final feature table contained 37 columns: an application identifier, the outcome, a recorded sex-code field retained for subgroup auditing, and 34 model predictors. I split the labelled sample into fixed stratified training (184,506), validation (61,502) and held-out test (61,503) partitions. Python workflows developed and evaluated the logistic and gradient-boosting models, and Tableau displays aggregate findings only.

The analysis has several limitations. The original source defines TARGET as a payment-difficulty outcome, not a certified default loss. Historical table coverage is incomplete, and unmatched historical parent records should not automatically be treated as invalid. The random held-out sample tests performance on historical data but does not provide an out-of-time prospective evaluation. The construction and lending-decision-time availability of external credit-related scores could not be independently verified. Subgroup differences are descriptive and do not establish discrimination. The dataset does not represent Kenya’s contemporary lending population, and retrospective review rankings should not be used as loan approval or rejection policies without further validation and governance.

Interpretation and responsible use. The analysis demonstrates a documented data-integration and predictive-modelling workflow. Reported metrics are retrospective and descriptive. It does not support individual lending decisions, causal claims or conclusions about Kenyan borrowers.
